Neon Tetra Care Guide

Paracheirodon innesi
✅ Beginner-friendly 🤝 Community fish 🐟 Schooling

The neon tetra is one of the world's most popular aquarium fish, and for good reason. Its electric blue stripe and red tail make it a showstopper in any planted community tank, and it's genuinely beginner-friendly when kept in proper conditions. There are an estimated 1.5 million neon tetras sold in the United States alone every month.

The key to keeping neon tetras successfully is understanding two things: they are schooling fish (never keep fewer than 6) and they prefer soft, slightly acidic water, which is the opposite of many tap water supplies.

Water Parameters

In the wild, neon tetras inhabit slow-moving, heavily shaded blackwater streams in the upper Amazon basin (Peru, Colombia, Brazil). The water is extremely soft, acidic (pH 4–5), dark with tannins, and cool. In the aquarium, they are more adaptable than wild conditions suggest, captive-bred neons tolerate pH up to 7.5 and moderate hardness, but they thrive in softer, slightly acidic conditions.

⚠️ Temperature matters for disease

Neon tetras are more susceptible to ich and Neon Tetra Disease at temperatures above 27 °C. Avoid overheating. If treating ich, raise temperature slowly to 28 °C maximum, not higher.

If your tap water is hard (above 15 dGH), consider blending with RO water to bring GH down to 5–8 dGH. Adding driftwood or Indian almond leaves naturally acidifies the water and releases tannins that neons love, you'll see brighter colours in soft, tannin-stained water.

Tank Size and Setup

Minimum tank: 40 litres (10 gallons) for a school of 6. But a 60–80 litre tank (15–20 gallons) is a far better choice, more water volume means more stable parameters, which directly reduces stress and disease in neon tetras.

Neon tetras are mid-water swimmers that love cover. The ideal setup includes:

  • Dense planting, Java fern, Anubias, and floating plants to dim the light and provide refuge
  • Dark substrate, dark sand or gravel makes their colours pop dramatically
  • Driftwood, releases tannins, slightly acidifies water, provides shelter
  • Low to moderate flow, they come from slow-moving water; strong flow stresses them
  • Subdued lighting, bright open tanks make neons hide; floating plants or a dimmer dramatically improves their behaviour
💡 Blackwater setup

For the best neon tetra colours, try a blackwater setup: dark substrate, driftwood, Indian almond leaves, and tannin-stained water (light tea colour). This closely mimics their natural habitat and neons kept this way show noticeably brighter blue stripes.

⚠️ Angelfish myth

You'll often see neon tetras and angelfish suggested as tank mates, they are not. Angelfish are natural predators of tetras in the wild and will eat neon tetras as soon as the angel is large enough to fit them in its mouth. This is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

Feeding

Neon tetras are omnivores with small mouths, they need micro-sized food. In the wild they eat tiny invertebrates, insect larvae, algae, and plant matter.

  • Micro pellets or nano flakes, the staple. Ensure the particle size is small enough for their tiny mouths (1–1.5 mm max)
  • Frozen or live micro foods, baby brine shrimp, micro worms, daphnia, and bloodworms greatly improve colour and condition
  • Blanched vegetables, occasionally offer blanched spinach or zucchini for added fibre

Feed once or twice daily, only what they can eat in 2–3 minutes. Overfeeding is a primary cause of poor water quality in neon tetra tanks. Remove any uneaten food promptly.

Schooling Behaviour

This cannot be overstated: neon tetras must be kept in a school. They are shoaling fish that have evolved to find safety in numbers. A lone neon tetra or a group of fewer than 6 will be chronically stressed, you'll see pale colours, hiding, and erratic swimming near the surface.

A school of 10–15 neon tetras shows dramatically different behaviour: coordinated shoaling, bold mid-water swimming, brilliant colours, and natural spawning attempts. Bigger schools are always better as long as your tank and filtration can support them.

🚨 Neon Tetra Disease, no cure

Neon Tetra Disease (NTD) is caused by a microsporidian parasite and there is currently no effective treatment. If you notice colour fading starting from the middle of the body, a curved spine, or raised cysts, remove the affected fish immediately to prevent spread to others. Never introduce new fish without a 2–4 week quarantine period.

Breeding

Breeding neon tetras in a home aquarium is possible but challenging, it requires a dedicated breeding tank with very specific conditions:

  • Dedicated 20–40 L breeding tank, very dim light, dark sides
  • Very soft, acidic water, pH 5.5–6.0, GH under 2 dGH (RO water or rainwater)
  • Temperature: 22–24 °C (cooler than the main tank to trigger spawning)
  • Fine-leaved plants or spawning mop, Java moss works well
  • Remove adults after spawning, neons eat their own eggs
  • Eggs hatch in 24–30 hours; fry are free-swimming after 3–4 days
  • First foods: infusoria, then baby brine shrimp as they grow

The main challenge is sourcing soft enough water. Most tap water is far too hard for neon tetra eggs to survive. Using 100% RO water with a small amount of peat-filtered water is the standard approach among breeders.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size tank do neon tetras need?

Neon tetras need a minimum 40-litre (10-gallon) tank to keep a school of 6. A 60–80 litre tank is better as it provides more swimming room and greater water stability. They are schooling fish and must never be kept alone or in groups of fewer than 6.

What water temperature do neon tetras need?

Neon tetras thrive at 22–26 °C (72–79 °F). They originate from cool blackwater streams in the Amazon basin and do not tolerate temperatures above 28 °C well. Aim for a stable 24–25 °C for most community setups.

Can neon tetras live with betta fish?

Sometimes, but with caution. Some betta fish tolerate neon tetras peacefully; others will nip at or eat them. If you try this combination, use a tank of at least 60 litres, provide plenty of plants and hiding spots, and monitor closely. Never add neon tetras to a tank with a betta that has shown aggression.

What is neon tetra disease?

Neon Tetra Disease (NTD) is caused by a microsporidian parasite (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis). It causes colour fading starting from the mid-body, a curved spine, and white cysts under the skin. There is no effective treatment, infected fish should be removed immediately to prevent spread. It enters tanks through infected live food or unquarantined new fish.

How many neon tetras should be kept together?

Neon tetras are schooling fish and must be kept in groups of at least 6. A school of 10–15 shows much more natural behaviour, bolder colours, and less stress. Keeping fewer than 6 causes chronic stress, pale colours, and excessive hiding.
